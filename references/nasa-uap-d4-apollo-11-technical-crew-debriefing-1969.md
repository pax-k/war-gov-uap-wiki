# NASA d4 — Apollo 11 Technical Crew Debriefing Excerpt (Vol I & II, July 31, 1969)

An **11-page Mistral-OCR'd excerpt** of the **APOLLO 11 / TECHNICAL / CREW DEBRIEFING (U)** dated **July 31, 1969**, prepared by the **Mission Operations Branch, Flight Crew Support Division, [[entities/nasa|NASA]] Manned Spacecraft Center (MSC) Houston**. The bundle covers transcript pages **6-33 through 6-39 (Vol. I)** and **21-1 through 21-2 (Vol. II §21.0 Visual Sightings)** — i.e., the **two parts of the debriefing where unidentified-object and visual-sighting content is concentrated**. The artifact is the **fourth** of seven NASA crew-transcript / debriefing files in the wiki's **d-cluster** (d1 [[references/nasa-uap-d1-apollo-12-transcript-1969|Apollo 12 voice loop]]; d2 Apollo 17 transcript pending; d3 Gemini 7 transcript; **d4 Apollo 11 — this artifact**; d5 [[references/nasa-apollo-17-science-debriefing-1973|Apollo 17 science]]; d6 [[references/nasa-apollo-17-technical-crew-debriefing-1973|Apollo 17 technical]]; d7 Skylab pending).

This is the **strongest astronaut-class signal** in the corpus to date. The excerpt contains the famous **"second look"** observation made approximately **one day before lunar arrival** by all three crewmen ([[entities/neil-armstrong|Armstrong]], [[entities/buzz-aldrin|Aldrin]], [[entities/michael-collins|Collins]]) of an unidentified luminous object that **resolved into a cylinder / two-rings / L-shape (open-suitcase) morphology** through the spacecraft's monocular and sextant — and was **specifically eliminated as the [[concepts/saturn-v-s-ivb-stage|S-IVB upper stage]]** by Mission Control ranging (*"6000 miles away"*). It also contains the corpus's **first 1969-vintage** cosmic-ray-light-flash testimony ([[entities/buzz-aldrin|Aldrin]]'s in-cabin "double flashes ... some sort of penetration ... entry and impact on something such as the struts" passage), establishing that the [[concepts/cosmic-ray-light-flashes|in-cabin cosmic-ray flash phenomenon]] was **observed and partially characterized by Aldrin three years before the Apollo 17 ALFMED experiment** instrumentation. ^[inferred]

## §6 — The "second look" observation (Vol I, pages 6-33 through 6-36)

The §6 transcript captures **Day 3 translunar coast** crew chatter (Aldrin's anchor: *"1 day out or something pretty close to the moon"*). The mission phase places the observation roughly **48 hours after S-IVB jettison** and **~24 hours before lunar orbit insertion** — i.e., approximately **18 July 1969 UTC**. ^[inferred]

The full transcript sequence (6-33 through 6-36) is reproduced and analyzed below. **All three crewmen speak.** The observation has multiple sequential phenomenology layers — visual acquisition through the cabin window, then through the monocular, then through the sextant in the LEB.

### Visual acquisition (6-33)

> **ALDRIN**: *"The first unusual thing that we saw I guess was 1 day out or something pretty close to the moon. It had a sizeable dimension to it, so we put the monocular on it."* ^[extracted]

> **COLLINS**: *"How'd we see this thing? Did we just look out the window and there it was?"* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"Yes, and we weren't sure but what it might be the S-IVB. We called the ground and were told the S-IVB was 6000 miles away."* ^[extracted]

The **first elimination is explicit and Mission-Control-confirmed**: the [[concepts/saturn-v-s-ivb-stage|Saturn V third stage (S-IVB)]] was **6,000 miles away** at the time of observation. ^[extracted] This is the single most important conventional-explanation closure in the entire corpus's orbital sub-class — every other partial-signal entry ([[references/nasa-uap-d1-apollo-12-transcript-1969|Apollo 12 d1]], [[references/sighting-gemini-7-bogey-1965-12-04|GT-7]]) leaves the spent-stage hypothesis open. ^[inferred]

### Audio-anomaly correlation (6-33)

> **ALDRIN**: *"We had a problem with the high gain about this time, didn't we?"* ^[extracted]

> **COLLINS**: *"There was something. We felt a bump or maybe I just imagined it."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"He was wondering whether the MESA had come off."* ^[extracted]

A **bump** sensation correlated **temporally with high-gain antenna anomalies**. Collins later (6-36) attributes the object's possible origin to the **spacecraft itself** on this basis (*"In the back of my mind, I have some reason to suspect that its origin was from the spacecraft"*). The **MESA** ([[entities/apollo-mesa|Modular Equipment Stowage Assembly]], a fold-out platform on the LM descent stage carrying the Hasselblad and TV camera ^[inferred]) was speculated as a possible loose-piece origin.

### Sequential morphology resolution (6-34 through 6-35)

> **ALDRIN**: *"Of course, we were seeing all sorts of little objects going by at the various dumps and then we happened to see this one brighter object going by. We couldn't think of anything else it could be other than the S-IVB. We looked at it through the monocular and it seemed to have a bit of an L shape to it."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"Like an open suitcase."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"We were in PTC at the time so each one of us had a chance to take a look at this and it certainly seemed to be within our vicinity and of a very sizeable dimension."* ^[extracted]

The crew was in **PTC** — [[concepts/passive-thermal-control|Passive Thermal Control]], the slow rotation maneuver Apollo CSM/LM stack performed for thermal balance during translunar coast. ^[inferred] PTC means **all three crewmen had sequential viewing geometry** — the spacecraft's rotation rotated each window past the object's bearing in turn. **This is structurally distinct from prior corpus orbital observations** ([[references/sighting-gemini-7-bogey-1965-12-04|GT-7 bogey]] is single-witness Borman; [[references/nasa-uap-d1-apollo-12-transcript-1969|Apollo 12 AOT]] is single-witness Bean) — the PTC viewing geometry made this the **first three-witness on-orbit UAP-adjacent observation** in the corpus. ^[inferred]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"We should say that it was right at the limit of the resolution of the eye. It was very difficult to tell just what shape it was. And there was no way to tell the size without knowing the range or the range without knowing the size."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"So then I got down in the LEB and started looking for it in the optics. We were grossly mislead because with the sextant off focus what we saw appeared to be a cylinder."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"Or really two rings."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"Yes."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"Two rings. Two connected rings."* ^[extracted]

> **COLLINS**: *"No, it looked like a hollow cylinder to me. It didn't look like two connected rings. You could see this thing tumbling and, when it came around end-on, you could look right down in its guts. It was a hollow cylinder. But then you could change the focus on the sextant and it would be replaced by this open-book shape. It was really weird."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"I guess there's not too much more to say about it other than it wasn't a cylinder."* ^[extracted]

The **morphology disagreements between crewmen** — Armstrong's "two rings", Collins's "hollow cylinder ... tumbling ... look right down in its guts", Aldrin's eventual *"it wasn't a cylinder"* — are **not contradictions** but rather **focus-dependent interpretations of an object near the resolution limit of the optical instruments**. ^[inferred] Aldrin later characterizes the cylinder reading as a **focus artifact** (*"with the sextant off focus what we saw appeared to be a cylinder"*) — i.e., the **cylinder/two-ring resolution is partly an instrument property, not purely an object property**. ^[inferred] The "open-book" / "open-suitcase" / "L-shape" reading appears with the sextant **in focus** and with the monocular at lower magnification.

### Conventional-explanation closure (attempted, 6-36)

> **COLLINS**: *"It was during the period when we thought it was a cylinder that we inquired about the S-IVB and we'd almost convinced ourselves that's what it had to be. But we don't have any more conclusions than that really. The fact that we didn't see it much past this one time period — we really don't have a conclusion as to what it might have been, how big it was, or how far away it was. It was something that wasn't part of the urine dump, we're pretty sure of that."* ^[extracted]

> **COLLINS** *(continuing)*: *"Skipping ahead a bit, when we jettisoned the LM, you know we fired an explosive charge and got rid of the docking rings and the LM went boom. Pieces came off the LM. It could have been some Mylar or something that had somehow come loose from the LM."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"We thought it could have been a panel, but it didn't appear to have that shape at all."* ^[extracted]

> **COLLINS**: *"That's right, and for some reason, we thought it might have been a part of the high gain antenna. It might have been about the time we had high gain antenna problems. In the back of my mind, I have some reason to suspect that its origin was from the spacecraft."* ^[extracted]

The **on-the-record crew conclusion** is **deliberately inconclusive**:

1. **S-IVB**: ruled out by Mission Control range data (*"6000 miles away"*).
2. **Urine dump particles**: ruled out by Collins (*"It was something that wasn't part of the urine dump, we're pretty sure of that"*).
3. **Panel from LM jettison debris**: tentatively offered by Collins, **rejected by Aldrin** (*"it didn't appear to have that shape at all"*) — and **chronologically incoherent**: the LM jettison occurred **after** lunar orbit on Day 8 of the mission, **5 days after the Day-3 observation**. ^[inferred] Collins offers this as a possible-origin hypothesis but the timing doesn't fit; the *"high gain antenna"* candidate is the more chronologically plausible spacecraft-origin candidate.
4. **High-gain antenna fragment**: tentatively offered by Collins as the most plausible spacecraft-origin candidate, given the contemporaneous high-gain anomaly. ^[extracted]

**The crew did not see it again.** This is consistent with a **passive ballistic co-ranging body** that diverged from the spacecraft's trajectory after a brief pass-by, but is **also consistent with** any one-time observation of any other class. ^[inferred]

## §21 — Visual Sightings (Vol II, pages 21-1 and 21-2)

§21 is the dedicated **Visual Sightings** section of Vol II. The Apollo 11 Vol II §21 excerpt is **2 pages** covering **§21.4 Translunar and Transearth Flight** and **§21.5 Lunar Orbit**. Notably, **§21 does not re-discuss the Day-3 cylinder observation** — that material is filed under the §6 *Translunar Operations* chapter in Vol I. §21 contains a different set of observations.

### §21.4 — Mars optical illusion (transearth coast)

> **ALDRIN**: *"There was only one minor observation returning from the Moon. Looking back at it, at a time after Mars had passed behind the Moon, there was one time period where I imagined that the image of Mars was coming from a region where it couldn't come from, because it was in a dark portion of the Moon. This obviously was an optical illusion of some sort."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"I suspect that it was, in fact, just immediately adjacent to the horizon."* ^[extracted]

This is **astronaut-self-attributed** as an **optical illusion** — Aldrin himself frames it as such, and Armstrong offers a candidate explanation (Mars **adjacent to the horizon**, not in front of dark lunar limb). **No UAP content.** ^[inferred]

### §21.5 — Lunar orbit "laser → reflection-off-a-lake" reattribution

> **ALDRIN**: *"In lunar orbit, following ascent, we did note and mention to the ground that approaching CDH when the Earth came up above the lunar horizon, I observed what appeared to be a fairly bright light source which we tentatively ascribed to a possible laser. That seemed to be the best possible explanation until we were coming back in the command module approaching the Earth and were able to observe something that gave about the same appearance. When putting the monocular on the light source, it appeared as though it was the reflection of the Sun from a relatively smooth body of water such as a lake. I think we've revised our initial conclusion as to what the source of that light was that we saw coming from the Earth. If no one owns up to having beamed the laser toward the Moon at that time, it was more probably a reflection off a lake. I still think it's an unusual phenomenon, at that distance, to see so bright a source of light. In the film, it didn't appear as though this was going to show up at all. The Earth was too bright."* ^[extracted]

This is the **only on-the-record astronaut-class report of a candidate ground-based laser shining at a lunar-orbiting spacecraft** — initially reported by Aldrin to Mission Control during lunar orbit (*"we did note and mention to the ground"*) but **post-flight self-reattributed** to **specular reflection of the Sun off a smooth body of water (a lake)** during transearth coast monocular observation of Earth. The reattribution is voluntary and Aldrin still flags the **brightness** as anomalous (*"I still think it's an unusual phenomenon, at that distance, to see so bright a source of light"*).

**Open question**: which lake. ^[open] The bearing implied is **on the visible Earth disk during Day 7 / Day 8** ascent-from-Moon-to-Earth phase — geographic narrowing to a specific lake would require the time-and-bearing data, which the excerpt does not preserve.

## §6 — Aldrin's in-cabin "flashes" / cosmic-ray penetration testimony (Vol I, pages 6-37 through 6-39)

The §6 transcript continues past the cylinder discussion into **Aldrin's in-cabin observation of light flashes**. This is a **separate phenomenon** from the Day-3 cylinder observation — it occurs **inside the cabin**, with the cabin lights out, while Aldrin is trying to sleep, and progresses across multiple nights of the mission.

> **ALDRIN**: *"The other observation that I made accumulated gradually. I don't know whether I saw it the first night, but I'm sure I saw it the second night. I was trying to go to sleep with all the lights out. I observed what I thought were little flashes inside the cabin, spaced a couple of minutes apart and I didn't think too much about it other than just note in my mind that they continued to be there."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN** *(cont'd)*: *"During transearth coast, we had more time and I devoted more opportunity to investigating what this could have been. It was at that point that I was able to observe on two different occasions that, instead of observing just one flash, I could see double flashes, at points separated by maybe a foot. At other times, I could see a line with no direction of motion and the only thing that comes to my mind is that this is some sort of penetration. At least that's my guess, without much to support it; some penetration of some object into the spacecraft that causes an emission as it enters the cabin itself."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN** *(cont'd)*: *"Sometimes it was one flash on entering. Possibly departing from an entirely different part of the cabin, outside the field of view. The double flashes appeared to have an entry and then impact on something such as the struts."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN** *(cont'd)*: *"For a while, I thought it might have been some static electricity because I was also able, in moving my hand up and down the sleep restraint, to generate very small sparks of static electricity. But there was a definite difference between the two as I observed it more and more."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN** *(cont'd)*: *"I tried to correlate this with the direction of the sun. When you put the window shades up there is still a small amount of leakage. You can generally tell within 20 or 30 degrees the direction of the sun. It seemed as though they were coming from that general direction; however, I really couldn't say if there was near enough evidence to support that these things were observable on the side of the spacecraft where the sun was. A little bit of evidence seemed to support this. I asked the others if they had seen any of these and, until about the last day, they hadn't."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"Buzz, I'd seen some light, but I just always attributed this to sunlight, because the window covers leak a little bit of light no matter how tightly secured. The only time I observed it was the last night when we really looked for it. I spent probably an hour carefully watching the inside of the spacecraft and I probably made 50 significant observations in this period."* ^[extracted]

> **ALDRIN**: *"Sometimes a minute or two would go by and then you'd see the two within the space of 10 seconds. On an average, I'd say just as a guess it was maybe something like one a minute. Certainly more than enough to convince you that it wasn't an optical illusion. It did give you a rather funny feeling to contemplate that something was zapping through the cabin. There wasn't anything you could do about it."* ^[extracted]

> **ARMSTRONG**: *"It could be something like Buzz suggested. Mainly a neutron or some kind of an atomic particle that would be in the visible spectrum."* ^[extracted]

This passage is the **wiki's first 1969 documentation of [[concepts/cosmic-ray-light-flashes|cosmic-ray-induced retinal light flashes]]**. The **interpretive framework Aldrin offers** — *"some sort of penetration ... entry and then impact on something such as the struts"* — and **Armstrong's closing line** — *"a neutron or some kind of an atomic particle that would be in the visible spectrum"* — together represent the **earliest astronaut-class statement of the correct physical model** for the phenomenon: **charged-particle traversal of the eye**, three years before [[references/nasa-apollo-17-technical-crew-debriefing-1973|Apollo 17's d6 ALFMED]] experimental investigation. ^[inferred]

The structural significance is large:

1. **Aldrin's "double flashes ... at points separated by maybe a foot"** describes a **chord-shaped trajectory** of a single particle traversing **two different points on the retina** as it passes through the eye — physically consistent with the [[concepts/cosmic-ray-light-flashes|standard cosmic-ray-on-retina model]]. ^[inferred] This phenomenology (entry + impact) is **directly observable** at the single-particle level and Aldrin captured it on first principles.
2. **Aldrin's "directionality ... seemed to be coming from the sun"** is **partially correct**: solar-event particles (during solar proton events) do show directional preference; **galactic cosmic rays** are nearly isotropic; the mix observed in cislunar space is dominated by the latter for normal solar conditions. Aldrin's directional inference is **a real signal he is fitting to incomplete data**. ^[inferred]
3. **Static-electricity rule-out**: Aldrin explicitly compares the in-cabin flashes to sleep-restraint static sparks and concludes *"there was a definite difference between the two"* — ruling out the most plausible mundane interpretation through observational discipline. ^[extracted]
4. **Last-night corroboration by Armstrong**: Armstrong's hour of careful observation (*"50 significant observations in this period"*) is **multi-witness empirical confirmation**, three years pre-ALFMED. ^[extracted]

## What the excerpt establishes

- **The Apollo 11 crew, in PTC during Day-3 translunar coast, made a multi-witness sequential-viewing-geometry observation of an unidentified object** through cabin window, monocular, and sextant. ^[extracted]
- **Mission Control range data eliminated the [[concepts/saturn-v-s-ivb-stage|S-IVB upper stage]]** as the candidate (range 6,000 miles at observation). ^[extracted]
- **The crew eliminated urine-dump particles, LM-jettison debris (chronologically incoherent), and panel from spacecraft body** as candidates; only **high-gain antenna fragment** survives as a plausible spacecraft-origin candidate. ^[extracted]
- **The crew did not reach a conclusion** on the object's identity, range, or size. ^[extracted]
- **All three crewmen (Armstrong, Aldrin, Collins) sequentially observed cylinder / two-ring / open-suitcase morphology**, with focus-dependent resolution between cylinder and L-shape readings. ^[extracted]
- **Aldrin observed in-cabin "double flashes" with directional structure** during the second night and across transearth coast, correctly hypothesized as **charged-particle penetration** of the spacecraft and the eye — three years before the ALFMED experiment. ^[extracted]
- **Armstrong corroborated Aldrin's flashes** on the last night with *"50 significant observations"* over an hour. ^[extracted]
- **Aldrin's lunar-orbit "laser" observation was self-reattributed** to specular sunlight reflection off a terrestrial lake; Aldrin still flagged the apparent brightness as anomalous. ^[extracted]
- **Aldrin's "Mars behind dark lunar limb" observation was self-attributed** as an optical illusion. ^[extracted]

## What the excerpt does *not* establish

- **No conclusive identification** of the Day-3 cylinder object. ^[extracted]
- **No follow-up observation** after the single Day-3 observation period. ^[extracted]
- **No range-rate / Doppler / radar / visual-tracking data** beyond Mission Control's S-IVB elimination range (*"6000 miles away"*). ^[extracted]
- **No specific time-of-observation timestamp** within Day 3 — Aldrin's *"1 day out or something pretty close to the moon"* anchors the phase but not the MET. ^[ambiguous]
- **No specific bearing data** — the observation is multi-window-geometry due to PTC, so a single bearing was not preserved. ^[inferred]
- **No film record**: Aldrin notes about the lunar-orbit "laser/lake" observation that *"In the film, it didn't appear as though this was going to show up at all. The Earth was too bright."* — by analogy, the Day-3 cylinder observation may also be missing from the 70mm/16mm DAC film record. ^[inferred] Verification requires Apollo 11 onboard photography and DAC film catalog cross-reference.
- **Whether NORAD or US Space Surveillance Network had a track on any object near the Apollo 11 spacecraft** at the Day-3 observation time. ^[open] The S-IVB range datum (*"6000 miles away"*) implies **Mission Control was tracking S-IVB but not necessarily other objects**.
