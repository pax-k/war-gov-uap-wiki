# Skylab 3 "Red Object" Co-Orbital Sighting (~Sep 1973)

The **Skylab 3 (SL-3) reddish co-orbital object observation** — a **multi-witness, ~10-minute, range-constrained, persistent, slowly-rotating bright reddish object** observed from the [[entities/skylab|Skylab]] orbital workshop's wardroom window approximately **a week before splashdown** (~September 1973 ^[inferred]) by **[[entities/alan-bean|Alan Bean]]** (CDR), **[[entities/owen-garriott|Owen Garriott]]** (Science Pilot), and **[[entities/jack-lousma|Jack Lousma]]** (Pilot). The observation is preserved in two parallel sections of the **[[references/nasa-uap-d7-skylab-technical-crew-debriefing-1973|d7 Skylab Technical Crew Debriefing]]** — the **§7 Visual Sightings (general)** and the **§20.0 Visual Sightings (dedicated chapter)**.

In the wiki's **[[concepts/orbital-uap-sighting|orbital-UAP-sighting]] typology**, this is the corpus's **first sub-class F entry**: **multi-witness + range-constrained + morphology-unresolved + conventional-candidate-not-eliminated**. The crew **treat the object as a satellite** throughout the testimony (*"obviously a satellite in a very similar orbit to our own"*, Garriott) but **never identify which satellite** and **never receive an explanation from the ground**. ^[extracted] The structural distinction from [[references/sighting-apollo-11-cylinder-1969-07|Apollo 11 d4 §6]] is critical: there, [[concepts/saturn-v-s-ivb-stage|S-IVB]] was **explicitly eliminated** by Mission Control range data; here, the conventional candidate (a different unidentified satellite) is **the crew's own prior, not eliminated**.

## Witness phenomenology (from §7 and §20)

The d7 excerpt preserves **two parallel narrative passes** of the same observation — the first in §7 (Bean, Lousma, Garriott exchange) and the second in §20.0 Visual Sightings (Lousma, Bean, Garriott exchange). Both passes are reproduced and analyzed below.

### §7 — first narrative pass (page 7-4 / 7-5)

> **GARRIOTT**: *"For example, we saw that satellite about a week before splash-down. That was one of the most unusual things that we saw and I guess Jack noticed it looking out the window. This bright reddish object was out there and we tracked it for about 5 or 10 minutes. It was obviously a satellite in a very similar orbit to our own. It was rotating and had a period of almost exactly 10 seconds because you could see the brightness vary with that period. We followed it until sunset and it went out of sunlight just about 5 to 7 seconds after we did. It held its position nearly the same, in the wardroom window for that 10-minute interval although we could see it drift in relative positions slightly, maybe 10 to - 20 degrees during the course of that 10-minute interval. It was reddish in color even when we were well above the horizon. As we approached sunset, it turned more reddish, presumably because of the sunlight change. What satellite it was and how it happened to end up in such a similar orbit, no one ever explained to us. And I would like to hear a few words from someone about that satellite."* ^[extracted]

> **BEAN**: *"You bet. We never saw it again. You'd think we would have seen it the next night or it would cycle by another time. Maybe it did and we weren't looking out the window."* ^[extracted]

> **LOUSMA**: *"You might point out that it never did take the shape of an object but it was always brighter than any other star or planet in the night sky. It was much brighter."* ^[extracted]

> **BEAN**: *"We tried monitors and everything on it but we could never make it into anything other than a bright light."* ^[extracted]

> **LOUSMA**: *"In doing T002, I had on other occasions, at least once or twice, seen other satellites although they appeared as star points of light."* ^[extracted]

### §20.0 — second narrative pass (page 20-1 / 20-2)

> **LOUSMA**: *"Powered Flight: I watched the booster protector cover go off and lots of flashes and debris and everything in every separation, but that's all normal. During orbit: -"* ^[extracted]

> **GARRIOTT**: *"Okay, about a week or 10 days before recovery and we were still waiting for information to be supplied to us about the identification. Jack first noticed this rather large red star out the wardroom window. Upon close examination, it was much brighter than Jupiter or any of the other planets. It had a reddish hue to it, even though it was well above the horizon. The light from the Sun was not passing close to the Earth's limb at the time. We observed it for about 10 minutes prior to sunset. It was slowly rotating because it had a variation in brightness with a 10-second period. As I was saying, we observed it for about 10 minutes, until we went into darkness, and it also followed us into darkness about 5-seconds later. From the 5- to 10-second delay in it's disappearance we surmised that it was not more than 30 to 50 nautical miles from our location. From it's original position in the wardroom window, it did not move more than 10 or 20 degrees over the 10 minutes or so that we watched it. It's orbit was very close to that of our own. We never saw it on any - earlier or succeeding orbits and we'd be quite interested in having its identification established. It's all debriefed in terms of time on channel A, so the precise timing and location can be picked up from there."* ^[extracted]

## Why this is sub-class F (and not A or E)

The d4 Apollo 11 ingest established the **A vs E distinction** as the corpus's most important orbital-class typological refinement. Skylab 3 d7 introduces a **new structural cell** that fits neither:

- **Differs from sub-class A** (GT-7 1965 Borman bogey): Skylab 3 has **three witnesses simultaneously** (vs. single-witness Borman); has **persistent tracking for ~10 minutes** (vs. single-bearing instantaneous call); has **range-constrained data** (~30–50 NM from sunset-shadow timing — vs. no range data on GT-7); has **a measured rotation period** (10 seconds — vs. no temporal structure on GT-7); has **directionality data** (~10–20° drift over 10 minutes — vs. single bearing on GT-7); and produced **a formal post-mission Mission Control identification request that went unanswered** (vs. GT-7's bogey-vs-booster ambiguity resolved on the same voice loop). ^[inferred] The **A-class booster-misidentification reading is not internally consistent** with this source record — Bean / Garriott / Lousma persistently observed for ten minutes from the wardroom window; whatever the object was, it was not the Skylab-launch [[concepts/saturn-v-s-ivb-stage|S-IVB]] (which was the Skylab-1 station itself, in their own orbit, not a free body 30–50 NM away). ^[inferred]

- **Differs from sub-class E** (Apollo 11 d4 1969 cylinder): Skylab 3 has **no morphology** (Lousma: *"it never did take the shape of an object"*; Bean: *"we could never make it into anything other than a bright light"*); has **no instrument-mediated optical resolution** beyond the wardroom window (the *"monitors and everything"* Bean refers to evidently failed to resolve a shape — different from Apollo 11's monocular-and-sextant resolution at multiple focal settings); and the conventional candidate (**another unidentified satellite in a very similar orbit**) is **NOT eliminated** but is in fact the crew's own working hypothesis. ^[inferred] What is missing from the conventional reading is **identification, not elimination**.

The proposed sub-class F operational definition:

> **Sub-class F — Positive (multi-witness, range-constrained, persistent, morphology-unresolved, conventional-candidate-not-eliminated).** Multi-witness simultaneous observation of an unidentified object on co-orbital trajectory. Range, rotation period, and angular drift derived from witness-side timing alone (no Mission Control ranging). Morphology unresolved across all available optical instruments. Conventional candidate (typically: another satellite or piece of orbital debris) is the crew's own prior; it is **not internally eliminated** but is **not externally identified** either. Crew explicitly request post-flight identification; identification is not received in the source record.

This is **not a "weaker E"** — it is a structurally distinct class. Sub-class E has the conventional candidate **explicitly eliminated by independent data** (Mission Control range); sub-class F has the conventional candidate **assumed but unidentified**. The two are different logical positions on the conventional-vs-anomalous-explanation axis.

## What the observation establishes

- **Three crewmen ([[entities/alan-bean|Bean]], [[entities/owen-garriott|Garriott]], [[entities/jack-lousma|Lousma]]) simultaneously observed a bright reddish object for ~10 minutes from the Skylab wardroom window** approximately 7–10 days before SL-3 splashdown. ^[extracted]
- **The object was co-orbital** with Skylab on a very similar orbital plane and altitude, derived from the persistence of its position in the wardroom window over a 10-minute interval. ^[extracted]
- **Range was ~30–50 nautical miles**, derived from the **5–7-second delay between Skylab's sunset-shadow ingress and the object's** (Garriott's geometric inference). ^[extracted]
- **The object had a 10-second rotation period**, derived from a regular variation in brightness. ^[extracted]
- **The object was reddish even when well above the horizon** (i.e., the reddish hue was not from atmospheric reddening of solar illumination through Earth's limb). ^[extracted]
- **The object was much brighter than Jupiter or any other planet or star in the night sky** when first observed, and turned more reddish as Skylab approached sunset. ^[extracted]
- **No morphology** was resolvable — *"it never did take the shape of an object"* through any instrument the crew tried. ^[extracted]
- **The crew explicitly requested post-mission identification of the object on channel A** and on the §20.0 debriefing record. ^[extracted]
- **The object was not seen again** on any earlier or succeeding orbit. ^[extracted]
- **Ground identification was not supplied** in the source record — the object remained unidentified through the date of the SL-3 technical debriefing (4 October 1973). ^[extracted]

## What the observation does *not* establish

- **No bearing data** in the OCR excerpt — only "in the wardroom window" geometry. The "channel A" voice-loop record may preserve the time-and-bearing for which Garriott points readers. ^[open]
- **No size estimate** — without morphology resolution, only the brightness and color are constrained. At 30–50 NM range, even a small (~1–2 m) co-orbital object can present as "much brighter than Jupiter" if specularly reflective. ^[inferred]
- **No spectroscopy / radiometry** — the reddish color is qualitative only.
- **No NORAD / US Space Surveillance Network correlation** preserved in the d7 excerpt. ^[open] The crew's request for identification implies they expected a NORAD / Mission-Control-side track to exist; that the request went unanswered implies either (a) no such track existed, (b) no such track was found, or (c) the track existed but was not communicated to the crew. The OCR cannot distinguish among these.
- **No thermal-class hypothesis** — solar-heating-of-rotating-debris reddening is plausible but not characterized. ^[inferred]

## Plausible identification candidates

The d7 OCR excerpt does not preserve any post-mission identification. Working candidates (each requires post-flight cross-reference, none constitute corpus claims):

1. **Co-launched Skylab debris**: Skylab launched 14 May 1973 on Saturn V SA-513; SL-3 launched 28 Jul 1973 on Saturn IB. By September 1973, both vehicles had jettisoned multiple stages, fairings, and shrouds. The S-II / S-IVB stages were placed into separation trajectories. ^[inferred] However, **[[entities/alan-bean|Bean]]'s remark *"we never saw it again ... you'd think we would have seen it the next night"*** weakens the simple co-launched-debris reading: passive co-orbital debris should remain in the orbital regime for many revolutions. ^[inferred]
2. **Russian Cosmos / Salyut-class object**: 1973 was an active Soviet orbital year (Salyut 2 reentered Sep 1973, Cosmos series launches throughout). A reentering / decaying co-orbital body could plausibly explain the brightness, reddish color (possibly from rapid heating in pre-reentry), and one-time-only visibility. ^[inferred]
3. **Another active satellite under classified observation**: 1973-era US KH-9 / KH-11 reconnaissance satellites operated in similar orbits. Whether any were within 30–50 NM of Skylab on the relevant date is not in the public record. ^[inferred]
4. **Reentering meteoroid**: A meteoroid entering atmosphere could produce reddish thermal emission and apparent co-orbital persistence over short windows, but the **10-minute observation duration** and **10-second rotation period** are inconsistent with typical bolide kinematics. ^[inferred]

The crew's working hypothesis (*"obviously a satellite"*) is plausible but not confirmed; the alternative hypotheses are also plausible but not confirmed. The source record permits the observation to remain **unidentified-but-not-anomalous**.

- **First [[entities/skylab|Skylab]]-era orbital UAP-adjacent observation** in the corpus.
- **First post-Apollo-program orbital UAP-adjacent observation** in the corpus (the prior NASA layer is GT-7 1965, Apollo 11 1969, Apollo 12 1969, Apollo 17 1972/3).
- **Corpus's first multi-witness sustained observation with explicit range estimate from witness-side data alone** — Apollo 11 d4's range elimination came from Mission Control; here the range estimate comes from the witnesses themselves via sunset-shadow timing. ^[inferred]
- **Bean's second corpus appearance** — first as Apollo 12 LMP on the d1 AOT particle-flash observation (sub-class D, [[references/nasa-uap-d1-apollo-12-transcript-1969]]), now as SL-3 CDR on this multi-witness sub-class F observation. **Bean's two corpus observations separate cleanly into "self-attributed conventional" (Apollo 12 AOT) and "unresolved-identification, multi-witness, range-data" (Skylab 3 red object)**. The same observer's two reports occupy two structurally distinct sub-classes — an **observational-discipline data point** that strengthens both. ^[inferred] (This parallels [[entities/buzz-aldrin|Aldrin]]'s d4 pattern: same Aldrin observed both the Apollo 11 cylinder (sub-class E unresolved) and the in-cabin cosmic-ray flashes (conventionally resolved). Bean and Aldrin are **the corpus's two most observationally-disciplined astronaut witnesses** under this metric. ^[inferred])
- **Closes a key open question on [[concepts/orbital-uap-sighting]]**: the typology's "modern-era" watch-for has been pending since the d4 ingest. SL-3 d7 supplies the first **post-Apollo** orbital instance and demonstrates that the d-cluster typology survives at least one mission generation past Apollo.
